I run two businesses and I am the only person in the operating layer of both.

JAK Labs is genuinely solo — no employees, no contractors, no one else. Haslett
Handyman has crews, because somebody has to be on the roof and it cannot be me
in two places at once. But everything above the work itself is one person:
scheduling, quoting, invoicing, payouts, collections, the software all of it
runs on, and the decision about what gets built next. There is no operations
manager. There is no bookkeeper. There is no second engineer.

That is the part people mean when they say "one-person company" — not the
absence of hands, the absence of a layer. Until this year it made me a slightly
odd freelancer.

Now there are a few million of me in China, and their government is paying for
the compute.

## What is actually happening

In the first half of 2025, China registered **2.86 million new one-person
companies** — a 47% jump year on year. Not freelancers: registered businesses,
run by a single person using AI to cover everything a team used to.

Then the state got behind it. In September the Ministry of Industry and
Information Technology folded one-person companies into the national
entrepreneurship support programme for 2026–2028. Provinces moved faster than
that. Guangdong is building ten "ecological communities" for them this year and a
hundred by 2028. Suzhou has pledged thirty communities and a thousand
one-person enterprises. Pudong covers computing costs up to 300,000 yuan — about
$44,000 a head. Wuhan offers loans where the state absorbs the default. Cities
are converting idle data centres and empty offices into free incubators.

Rest of World quoted a researcher, Lin Zhang, on why this looks so unlike
Silicon Valley: when a new technology appears, China mobilises "the entire
bureaucratic system." There is no venture capitalist in this story. There is a
policy.

## The number that should stop you

A think tank that tracked more than 500 of these companies found roughly **80%
fail within a year**.

Hold those two facts next to each other. Millions of registrations, real
subsidies, free compute, and the overwhelming majority are gone inside twelve
months.

That is not an argument that AI does not work. I use these tools every day and
they do exactly what they claim. It is an argument about what they work *on*.

## The thing AI did not change

AI collapsed the cost of producing almost anything. Code, copy, images, video,
analysis — the marginal cost of making a competent version of nearly any
artefact fell through the floor, and it fell for everyone simultaneously.

It did nothing at all to the cost of finding somebody who will pay you.

Those are two different businesses. The first is production. The second is
demand. Every one-person company that fails inside a year failed at the second
one, and no amount of the first fixes it — a solo operator with world-class
production capability and no customers is a solo operator with no customers,
now with better tooling and a subsidised GPU.

Read the reporting and you can see it plainly. Rest of World profiled Ma
Ruipeng, 41, who left a twenty-year programming career three months earlier to
build AI software for making mobile apps. He works in Claude Code, Figma and
OpenClaw — genuinely capable tools, the same ones I use. His earnings so far:
none. He is living off savings.

He is not incompetent. He is a competent engineer who has solved production and
has not yet solved demand, and the state has handed him a subsidy for the half
he had already solved.

## Where this came from, which matters

Much of the enthusiastic coverage is state media. Coverage of a policy, by
outlets attached to the government running it, is not proof the policy works.

The context those pieces mention less: youth unemployment in China is running at
roughly **one in six** for 16- to 24-year-olds. Domestic consumer demand is
weak. Wei Xin, a 34-year-old document reviewer at a consulting firm, told
reporters she knew AI would take her job before it did — so she took a Gemini
course, built an AI version of herself, and moved into content.

Sponsoring one-person companies is a cheap answer to that. Cheaper than jobs
programmes, and it reframes a labour-market problem as an entrepreneurship
opportunity. Baiguan put the question better than I can: is this an
entrepreneurial revolution, or an elaborate coping mechanism for a labour market
under stress?

I do not think it is only one of those. Both can be true at once, and the 80%
number is what it looks like when they are.

## What I did differently, and it was not skill

I want to be careful here, because the easy version of this post is a solo
operator congratulating himself while a few million people fail.

I did not start a one-person company. I had a business first, and then became
the only person running it.

The handyman company existed. It had customers, invoices, crews and a scheduling
problem, and I wrote software because the business was drowning in admin. To
date **$216K has been invoiced** through software I wrote and still operate, and
**$192K collected**, across **253 jobs** carried end to end, with **51 scheduled
automations** running the parts nobody should be doing by hand.

Every one of those numbers is downstream of demand that already existed. The
crews exist because customers do. The software exists because I could not hire
an operations team and would not have wanted to — it is what lets one person
carry scheduling, billing and payouts for a business with people in the field.

None of it conjured anybody who wanted their gutters cleaned.

That is the whole difference, and it is a difference of sequence, not talent. I
solved demand first — badly, slowly, by doing the work — and applied leverage
second. The structure being subsidised across China inverts that: leverage
first, demand later, hopefully.

Sequence is not a small detail. It is most of the outcome.

## What I would take from this if I were starting

**Whatever AI makes cheap is not your moat.** If a tool let you do it this
morning, it let several million other people do it this morning. Production
capability was scarce, so it felt like an advantage. It is now the floor.

**Find the demand before you build the leverage.** The least glamorous version
of this works: sell the thing by hand, badly, to one real customer who pays.
Then automate what hurt. That order is slower to start and far harder to kill.

**Subsidy is not demand.** Free compute, a free desk and a state-backed loan
reduce the cost of existing. None of them is a customer. A business that is
alive because the cost of being alive was lowered has not been tested yet.

**The bottleneck was never making the thing.** This is the part I would most
want somebody starting out to hear, because it is the opposite of how the tools
are sold. What nearly killed my business was not an inability to produce —
plenty of people can swing a hammer or write a function. It was scheduling,
quoting, chasing invoices, splitting crew pay, and the fact that all of it ran
through one head. Admin does not scale by working harder, it scales by being
carried by something that does not get tired.

That is the actual gift here, and it is quieter than the demos: AI did not make
me able to build things. It made me able to be the entire operating layer of a
business that has people in the field, without hiring a layer of managers to do
it. That is real, and it is worth more than another content generator.

**Being small is a real advantage, just not the one being sold.** One person can
carry an entire operation now, which used to be impossible. That is genuinely
new and it is why my business works. But it is an advantage in *serving* demand,
not in *finding* it.

## What I will actually be watching

Not the registration counts. Those measure how many people took the offer.

The number that would change my mind is revenue per surviving company after the
subsidies end, two or three years out. If a meaningful share of these are still
running on customer money once the free compute stops, then something genuinely
new has happened to the shape of a business and I will say so.

If the survivors are mostly the ones who had customers before the subsidy, then
this was a very large and very expensive demonstration of something already
well understood — and the people who learned it are a few million young Chinese founders
who deserved better than to learn it this way.

I do not know which. Nobody does yet. It is the most interesting live experiment
in my industry, and I am, in a small way in Michigan, one of its data points.
