A careers list went round recently — ten jobs that will dominate the next ten
years. I am going to take all ten seriously, one post each, because lists like
this get shared far more than they get examined.

I should say up front which ones I actually hold: AI automation, fractional
executive, infrastructure, and trades business owner. Four of ten. For the rest
I will be explicit about where I am reasoning from adjacent experience and where
I am guessing.

This one I hold by function and not by title.

## What the title suggests

Someone who decides which AI features get built. Prioritises a roadmap, writes
specs, talks to users, ships models into products.

## What it actually is

Deciding what not to build, repeatedly, against enormous pressure to build it.

I am the product manager of my own systems, and the decisions I am proudest of
are almost all deletions. There is a line in my own project notes that reads
**"do not build a fourth importer."** I wrote it after noticing that the CRM had
three working importers, a pipeline holding thousands of leads, and almost none
of them ever contacted.

The fourth importer would have been satisfying to build. It would have taken a
day, worked perfectly, and moved the business precisely nowhere, because the
constraint was never getting leads in. It was that nobody was working them.

That is the actual job. Not choosing between features — choosing between the
feature you want to build and the unglamorous thing that is genuinely blocking
you. They almost never feel the same at the moment of decision.

## Why AI makes this harder, not easier

The old constraint on building the wrong thing was cost. Building took weeks, so
somebody asked whether it was worth weeks. That question was doing enormous
quiet work, and it was doing it for free.

When building takes an afternoon, nobody asks. The idea arrives, the tool makes
it real before the doubt catches up, and you end up with a system full of things
that work and do not matter.

I have shipped that system. The way out was not better prioritisation
frameworks. It was writing down, in the repository, the thing the system is
actually short of — and making the next person read it before they build.

## What I would look for in someone doing this job

Not a roadmap. Ask them what they killed this quarter and why, and listen for
whether the reason is about the user or about the team's capacity. Ask what
their product is currently short of, and see whether the answer is a feature.

If someone tells you their product is short of a feature, they have usually not
looked hard enough. Most products are short of users who know they exist.

## Where I come into this

I spend most of my time on this decision for other people's businesses — what belongs in software, what belongs in a rule, and what should not be built at all. If you have a backlog and a suspicion that the top of it is wrong, [tell me what you are building](/contact). If the honest answer is that your roadmap is fine and the problem is distribution, I will say that instead.
